Sell more than you generate and you buy the shortfall back at the imbalance price; sell less and the surplus settles cheap. The optimal commitment therefore sits below your median forecast. How an edition works

  1. 01

    Qualify

    Participants receive a curated dataset built from open grid data and a working baseline model. A qualifying round on historical periods filters the field and opens the leaderboard.

  2. 02

    Commit, daily

    Before each gate closure, every participant submits a probabilistic forecast and a position, then adjusts it in intraday as the forecast sharpens. Submissions are timestamped server-side and locked. Nobody sees the outcome before committing.

  3. 03

    Settle on realised prices

    Positions settle against published day-ahead, intraday and imbalance prices. The score is the share of perfect-foresight revenue captured, which is the benchmark a battery desk already uses internally.

  4. 04

    Meet the top of the board

    Sponsors receive the ranked pool with full performance histories and speak to whoever they want. Participants compete under a pseudonym and choose when to reveal themselves.

How an edition is scored

Foretec · edition one, DK1 · draft for discussion, September 2026

A participant holds a notional 100 MW share of DK1 onshore wind. They commit a position ahead of delivery, revise it once as the forecast sharpens, and carry whatever is left as imbalance. The score is the share of perfect-foresight revenue they capture. Everything settles against published prices; the only modelled quantity is what a participant's own volume would have done to the price, because that is the one thing no dataset contains. Every choice below trades realism against the number of people who can enter, and this is where the line is drawn.

The asset

HEFTCom used Hornsea 1 and lost time to an unannounced outage the organisers spotted late; participants had no instructions on where to read REMIT notices. Using a fixed share of DK1 aggregate onshore wind removes that entire failure mode. There is no single unit to trip, no outage feed to monitor, and the underlying series is published by Energinet without a licence negotiation. Managing a portfolio rather than one asset is also closer to what a desk actually does.

The cost is a little realism: zonal wind is smoother than a single farm, so forecast errors are smaller in percentage terms and the problem is marginally easier. That is an acceptable price for a first edition that has to start on schedule.

Submissions

Participants never place orders. For each delivery quarter they state the net position they want to hold after each gate, and the engine differences consecutive positions to recover the volume traded at that gate. Nobody models fills, queue priority or order lifetime, and someone who has never touched a power market can submit a valid entry as one column of numbers.

There are two gates. The day-ahead commitment closes at 12:00 on D−1; a single intraday revision closes one hour before delivery. One revision captures most of the forecast-error decay between day-ahead and delivery — further gates add infrastructure and submission burden for very little additional measured skill, and can be added in a later edition if the field asks for them.

Settlement

Each leg gets the treatment its market actually justifies, and each assumption is stated rather than buried. Day-ahead is a uniform-price auction where a marginal volume genuinely does not move the clearing price, so that leg needs no correction and no apology. Intraday is continuous trading against an order book, so the published index is a mid that nobody transacts at: crossing the spread is a real cost and the edition charges it. Imbalance is the only leg where a participant's volume plausibly moves the price they themselves receive, and it is the only leg carrying a modelled term.

Day-ahead

xDA × πDA

Published day-ahead price, no impact term. Since the move to 15-minute market time units the day-ahead product matches the imbalance settlement period, so all three legs sit on the same 96-row grid.

Intraday

(xID − xDA) × (πID − sgn · s/2)

Published intraday index, with half the spread charged against the direction traded: you sell below the index and buy above it. Calibrate s from observed DK1 trade dispersion around the index, publish the number and the method.

Imbalance

(y − xID) × (πIMB − λt · (y − xID))

Published imbalance price, less a linear impact term. λt varies with how tight the period was, as set out under Price impact below.

Price impact

The published imbalance price is the price that occurred given the system imbalance that actually happened — and that imbalance did not include the participant's notional wind. The data holds one realisation, not a function mapping volume to price, so there is no feed that fixes this. It is a missing counterfactual, not a missing dataset.

Leaving it out is not neutral either. Against a fixed price path with no impact, any period where the imbalance price beat day-ahead has an obvious answer: commit nothing and take the whole volume at imbalance. That is a corner solution, and a leaderboard full of them ranks people on a price read rather than on forecasting and decision skill. HEFTCom prevented it with a constant coefficient of 0.07, which their paper states but never derives or calibrates.

The proposal here keeps the linear form — it is cheap, it keeps every participant's score independent of every other participant's, and it is reproducible from published data — but lets the slope vary with how tight the period was, using the period's imbalance-to-day-ahead spread against its running median, clipped to a bounded range. The base level is then justified by the job it does: it is set at the level that makes deliberate imbalance unprofitable in backtest. That is an honest account of a free parameter, and it is a stronger defence than an uncalibrated constant.

Two tracks

The clearest warning from HEFTCom is that the trading task deterred people: almost nobody entered who was not already working in energy. The fix is not to remove trading but to stop making it the entry requirement. The required column is a position, scored on captured value. The optional quantile columns are scored on pinball loss as a separate table. A forecaster enters with the columns they already understand, sees the value track running beside them, and can step across in a later round.

Both tracks then feed a third entrant. Every submission that clears the baseline is combined into one aggregate — a median position on the value track, and a quantile-by-quantile median on the forecast track — which is scored under its own name on the public leaderboard alongside everyone else. This is not decoration. Combined forecasts routinely beat almost every individual that goes into them, and the aggregate is scored on the same realised prices with the same impact term, so a participant who finishes above it has cleared a bar that most of the field did not. It is also the artefact a sponsor keeps after the edition closes, which is why it is built from the field rather than from the top ten.

Calendar

HEFTCom ranked once, over the whole run, and teams who made an early error dropped out because the average was unrecoverable. Scoring in fortnightly rounds with points and a small prize per round, plus an overall table, keeps somebody in the game after a bad week and gives a sponsor something to watch every two weeks rather than once at the end.

The qualifying round does double duty. It filters the field on historical periods before any live money is committed, and it identifies the teams worth funding: a modest participation grant to the teams that qualify, following the model used by the US power system operation competitions, buys real effort from people who would otherwise treat this as a weekend project.

Open parameters

Nothing below is a research question. Each is a value to be chosen, backtested on a season of DK1 data, and then published with the reasoning attached — which is the whole difference between a defensible design and a set of magic constants.
